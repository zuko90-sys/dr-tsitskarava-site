Gastritis and H. pylori

Helicobacter pylori, or H. pylori, is the main cause of chronic gastritis and ulcers. If it's found, it gets treated. But it's important to get it right the first time: every failed attempt leaves fewer antibiotics that still work.

Who should be tested for H. pylori

  • if you have pain, heaviness or feel full quickly in the upper abdomen;
  • if you have or have had a stomach or duodenal ulcer;
  • if you take aspirin or NSAID painkillers long term;
  • if you have iron deficiency anemia and no cause has been found;
  • if a parent, brother or sister has had stomach cancer.

How testing works and how to prepare

The reliable methods are a breath test and a stool antigen test. If you need an upper endoscopy (gastroscopy) for other reasons, the bacteria are looked for in a biopsy. A blood antibody test only shows that you've come across the bacteria at some point, so it's no use for checking whether treatment worked.

To avoid a false negative result, acid-reducing medications are stopped 2 weeks before the test, and antibiotics and bismuth medications 4 weeks before.

How we treat it

I choose the treatment regimen following the Maastricht VI and ACG guidelines, and I always ask which antibiotics you've taken before, because that affects whether the treatment will work. The course lasts 14 days. 4 weeks after it ends, we test again to see whether the bacteria are gone. Without this follow-up test, there's no way to say the treatment worked.

When you need an upper endoscopy (gastroscopy)

Not everyone with stomach pain. Under the ACG and Canadian Association of Gastroenterology guidelines, people under 60 with no particular risk factors are first tested for H. pylori and treated if it's found. I recommend an endoscopy if you have anything from this list:

  • swallowing is difficult or painful, or food “gets stuck”;
  • you're losing weight without trying;
  • repeated vomiting, blood in your vomit, black stools;
  • your blood tests show iron deficiency anemia;
  • a parent, brother or sister has had stomach or esophageal cancer;
  • your symptoms first appeared after age 60.

Frequently asked questions

Does H. pylori need treatment if nothing hurts?+

According to the Maastricht VI consensus, yes: once the infection is found, it's treated, because it raises the risk of ulcers and stomach cancer. We discuss the timing and the regimen at your visit.

Should my family get tested?+

If a close relative has had stomach cancer, getting tested is a must. In other cases, we decide individually.

Do I need a special diet for gastritis?+

Not a strict, lifelong one; there's no evidence it helps. It's more important to find and remove the cause: H. pylori, painkillers, smoking.
