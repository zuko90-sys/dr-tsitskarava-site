Bloating and gas

Bloating is one of the most common reasons people come to see me, usually after months of experimenting with food on their own. The menu keeps getting shorter, and the bloating stays the same. The cause can almost always be found, and that's the best place to start.

Where bloating comes from

Everyone has gas in the gut: bacteria produce it as they break down fiber and some carbohydrates. You feel bloated when there's a lot of gas or when your gut reacts to it too strongly. In practice, the most common culprits are:

  • carbohydrates that ferment quickly in the gut (known as FODMAPs): legumes, onions, garlic, wheat, some fruits, and sweeteners ending in -itol;
  • lactose, if you don't tolerate it well;
  • irritable bowel syndrome, where the gut is more sensitive than usual;
  • constipation: gas gets trapped along with the stool;
  • the abdominal and diaphragm muscles not working properly: the belly pushes outward even though there's no more gas than normal.

Less often, the cause turns out to be celiac disease, bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine, or medications.

When to see a doctor without delay

Bloating on its own is rarely dangerous. But if you have it along with even one of the following, you should get checked soon:

  • blood in your stool;
  • you're losing weight without trying;
  • your blood tests show iron deficiency anemia;
  • pain or diarrhea wakes you up at night;
  • you have a fever that won't go away;
  • a close relative has had bowel cancer, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis or celiac disease;
  • your symptoms started after age 45: from that age, everyone is advised to be screened for bowel cancer.

Women over 50 who feel bloated almost every day for more than a few weeks should have their ovaries checked. This is what the UK's NICE guidelines advise.

How I look for the cause

First I ask you a lot of questions: when you get bloated, after which foods, how often you go to the toilet and what your stool is like, and what medications and supplements you take. Sometimes I ask you to keep a food and symptom diary for 1–2 weeks; it often tells us more than any test. I only order breath tests and other investigations when they could change the treatment.

What helps

Treatment depends on the cause. If food is the problem, a low-FODMAP diet can help, but only as a short experiment for a few weeks. Then foods are brought back one at a time to see which ones actually cause the bloating. In the end, few restrictions remain, and often none at all. If the cause is constipation, we treat the constipation. If it's how the abdominal and diaphragm muscles work, biofeedback exercises help.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need any tests before the visit?+

No. Bring any results you already have, even old ones. We'll decide together what else is needed.

Will probiotics help?+

The American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) advises against probiotics for bloating: there's no convincing evidence that they help. Your money is better spent on finding the cause.

Can we work on this online?+

Yes. In an online consultation I go through your symptoms, diet and test results in detail and give you a written plan. If you need a physical exam, I'll tell you.
