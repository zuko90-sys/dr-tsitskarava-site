Heartburn and reflux

If heartburn gets in the way of your life and comes back once a week or more often, it may be gastroesophageal reflux disease, or GERD. It responds well to treatment, and you hardly need a list of “forbidden” foods for that.

What happens in reflux

Stomach contents flow back up into the esophagus. This happens to everyone, but with GERD it happens too often, or the esophagus has become too sensitive. Reflux is more likely with excess weight, a heavy dinner before bed, smoking, a hiatal hernia and pregnancy.

Besides a burning feeling behind the breastbone, you may have sour burping and a feeling that food is coming back up into your mouth. Coughing, hoarseness and a lump in the throat are sometimes linked to reflux too, but without heartburn this has to be confirmed by tests first. With chest pain, we always start by checking the heart.

When you need an upper endoscopy (gastroscopy)

If your heartburn is typical and there are no warning signs, the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) guidelines say treatment can start right away. An endoscopy is needed if treatment hasn't helped within 8 weeks, or if you have anything from this list:

  • swallowing is difficult or painful, or food “gets stuck”;
  • you're losing weight without trying;
  • repeated vomiting, blood in your vomit, black stools;
  • your blood tests show iron deficiency anemia;
  • a parent, brother or sister has had stomach or esophageal cancer;
  • your symptoms first appeared after age 60.

How we treat it

Simple things work: losing excess weight, having dinner 2–3 hours before bed and, if heartburn bothers you at night, raising the head of your bed. We cut out only the foods that trigger heartburn for you, rather than the whole “acidic and spicy” list in advance.

I prescribe medication as a course, usually for 8 weeks, and explain how to take it so that it works. Once the symptoms are gone, we lower the dose together to the lowest one that still works, or try stopping it.

Frequently asked questions

Is it harmful to take heartburn pills for a long time?+

GERD medications have been studied for decades. Links to side effects come mainly from observational studies and are still debated. If you need the medication, the benefits outweigh the risks. If you don't, it's worth stopping, and that's something we discuss at your visit.

Heartburn in pregnancy: what can I take?+

Heartburn is common in pregnancy. We start with diet and daily routine, and I only choose medications that are safe during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

Can we work on this online?+

Yes. Online, I go through your symptoms and test results and give you a written plan. If you need an endoscopy, I'll suggest where to have it done.
